Diabetes: An Overview

Overview

Diabetes is a chronic condition that affects how your body turns food into energy. Most food is broken down into sugar (glucose) and released into the bloodstream, where insulin — a hormone made by the pancreas — helps move it into cells for energy. With diabetes, the body doesn't make enough insulin, doesn't use it well, or both, so blood sugar stays too high over time.

Left unmanaged, consistently high blood sugar (hyperglycemiaHyperglycemiaBlood sugar that is higher than normal, which over time can damage blood vessels and nerves.
See full glossary →) can damage blood vessels and nerves throughout the body. This is why early diagnosis and ongoing management matter — not just for day-to-day symptoms, but for long-term health.

Diabetes was first described thousands of years ago, but it wasn't until the discovery of insulin in the early 1920s that it became a manageable condition rather than a fatal one for people with type 1 diabetes. Since then, treatment has advanced dramatically, from animal-derived insulin to today's synthetic insulins, continuous glucose monitorsContinuous glucose monitor (CGM)A wearable sensor that tracks blood sugar levels throughout the day and night, without repeated finger pricks.

Blood sugar regulation is a balancing act involving several hormones, not just insulin. Glucagon, produced by the pancreas as well, raises blood sugar when it's too low, working opposite to insulinInsulinA hormone made by the pancreas that helps move sugar from the bloodstream into the body's cells for energy.

Common Symptoms

  • Increased thirst and frequent urination
  • Fatigue
  • Blurred vision
  • Slow-healing sores or cuts
  • Unexplained weight loss (more common in type 1)
  • Tingling or numbness in hands or feet
  • Increased hunger, even after eating
  • Frequent infections, such as gum, skin, or vaginal infections
  • Dry, itchy skin
  • Darkened patches of skin, often around the neck or armpits
  • Irritability or mood changes tied to blood sugar swings
  • Headaches, particularly when blood sugar is very high or very low

One in three American adults today is impacted in some way by diabetes or related health concerns, making it one of the most talked-about conditions in the country.

Risk Factors

Several factors can raise the likelihood of developing type 2 diabetes, including having a family history of the condition, being over age 45, carrying extra weight, being physically inactive, and having had gestational diabetes in a previous pregnancy. Some racial and ethnic groups also face higher risk, though the exact reasons are still an active area of study.

For type 1 diabetes, the causes are different and less well understood — genetics play a role, and researchers believe environmental triggers, such as certain viral infections, may contribute in people who are already genetically susceptible.

Other contributing factors for type 2 risk include high blood pressure, abnormal cholesterol levels, polycystic ovary syndrome, and a history of heart disease or stroke. Sleep quality has also emerged as an area of interest, with some research linking chronic poor sleep to higher insulin resistance.

How Diabetes Is Diagnosed

Healthcare providers typically use one of a few blood tests to diagnose diabetes: a fasting blood sugar test, an A1C testA1CA blood test that reflects your average blood sugar level over roughly the past three months.
See full glossary → that reflects average blood sugar over about three months, or an oral glucose tolerance test. Because symptoms can be mild or easy to miss early on, routine screening is often recommended for people with risk factors, even before symptoms appear.

If your results are borderline, your provider may repeat testing on a different day to confirm the diagnosis before starting treatment.

For gestational diabetes specifically, most pregnant individuals are screened between 24 and 28 weeks using a glucose challenge test, though those at higher risk may be tested earlier.

The good news is that with today's understanding of diabetes, more people than ever are able to catch it early and take steps toward better health.

Prevention

While type 1 diabetes cannot currently be prevented, research suggests that type 2 diabetes can often be delayed or prevented through sustained lifestyle changes, including maintaining a healthy weight, staying physically active, and eating a balanced diet. Structured lifestyle-change programs have shown meaningful results for people with prediabetes.

Even modest changes — such as losing a small percentage of body weight or adding regular walking — have been associated with meaningfully lower progression rates from prediabetes to type 2 diabetes in long-term studies.

Treatment Options

Treatment depends on the type of diabetes and individual health needs. Type 1 diabetes always requires insulin, delivered through injections or an insulin pumpInsulin pumpA small wearable device that delivers a steady, adjustable flow of insulin throughout the day.
See full glossary →. Type 2 diabetes treatment often starts with lifestyle changes and may progress to oral medications, non-insulin injectables, or insulin if blood sugar isn't well controlled through other means.

Newer classes of medication can also support weight management and offer cardiovascular benefits for some people with type 2 diabetes, which is why treatment plans are increasingly personalized rather than one-size-fits-all.

Diabetes by the Numbers

Overview

Studies show that nearly 9 out of 10 Americans could improve their health by understanding diabetes better, making this one of the most important conditions to learn about today.

Diabetes affects millions of people, and the number continues to rise every year across all age groups.

Who Is Affected

Diabetes touches nearly every family in some way. Older adults are more likely to be affected than younger people, and men and women are affected at roughly similar rates. Many people living with prediabetes don't yet know it.

Rates also vary by region and community, which is part of why public health researchers continue to study the social and environmental factors that influence diabetes risk.

Globally, diabetes rates have been rising alongside urbanization and changing dietary patterns, though the pace and drivers of that rise differ significantly from country to country.

Economic Impact

Diabetes and its complications cost the country an enormous amount each year in medical care and lost productivity — some estimates put the number in the hundreds of billions of dollars annually.

These costs include direct medical expenses like hospital stays, medications, and supplies, as well as indirect costs such as missed workdays and reduced productivity.

Out-of-pocket costs for people managing diabetes can also be substantial, particularly for insulin and testing supplies, which is why insurance coverage and cost-assistance programs are frequently discussed in public health policy.

Complications Over Time

Long-term high blood sugar can contribute to serious complications, including:

  • Heart disease and stroke
  • Kidney disease, which can progress to kidney failure
  • Diabetic retinopathyDiabetic retinopathyDamage to blood vessels in the retina caused by high blood sugar, a leading cause of vision loss in adults with diabetes.
    See full glossary →    , a leading cause of vision loss in adults
  • Nerve damage (neuropathy)NeuropathyNerve damage caused by prolonged high blood sugar, often affecting sensation in the feet and hands.
    See full glossary →    , often affecting the feet and hands
  • Slow wound healing, which can lead to serious foot complications
  • Increased risk of certain infections
  • Hearing impairment, which some research links to diabetes
  • Higher risk of depression and anxiety related to chronic disease management

Regular checkups can catch many of these complications early, when they're most treatable.

Trends

Diagnoses have been trending upward for years, a pattern researchers link to factors like aging populations and lifestyle changes, though awareness and early screening have also improved.

Public health officials often track these trends to guide funding for prevention and treatment programs.

Managing Diabetes

Management typically involves monitoring blood sugar, taking prescribed medication or insulin, eating balanced meals, staying active, and regular checkups to watch for complications affecting the eyes, kidneys, nerves, and heart.

With the right care, almost anyone can live a full and active life while managing diabetes successfully.

Resources

Getting Support

Living with diabetes can feel overwhelming at first, but a range of support options can help, from diabetes education programs to online communities and in-person support groups. Many people find that connecting with others managing the same condition makes a real difference.

Diabetes self-management education programs, often led by certified educators, can teach practical day-to-day skills like carbohydrate countingCarbohydrate countingA meal-planning method that tracks grams of carbohydrate eaten, helping match food intake to insulin doses or activity levels.
See full glossary →, reading nutrition labels, and adjusting medication around exercise.

Family members and caregivers can also benefit from education, particularly for children newly diagnosed with type 1 diabetes, since daily management often becomes a shared responsibility.

Diet and Exercise Basics

There's no single "diabetes diet," but many care teams recommend an eating pattern rich in vegetables, whole grains, lean proteins, and healthy fats, while paying attention to portion sizes and carbohydrate intake. Working with a registered dietitian can help tailor an approach to individual needs and preferences.

Regular physical activity helps the body use insulin more effectively and can improve blood sugar control over time. Most guidance suggests a mix of aerobic activity and strength training, adjusted to individual ability and any existing complications.

Questions to Ask Your Doctor

  • What type of diabetes do I have, and what does that mean for my treatment?
  • What should my target blood sugar range be?
  • How often should I check my blood sugar?
  • Are there any warning signs I should watch for?
  • What lifestyle changes would help most in my situation?
  • How might diabetes affect other health conditions I have?
  • What should I do if my blood sugar is too high or too low?

Frequently Asked Questions & Contact

Frequently Asked Questions

Can diabetes be cured?
There's currently no cure, but many people are able to manage it so well that it barely affects their daily life.

Is diabetes contagious?
No. Diabetes is not contagious and cannot be spread from person to person.

Can children get diabetes?
Yes. Type 1 diabetes is most often diagnosed in children and young adults, and type 2 diabetes, once rare in kids, has become more common in recent years.

Does eating sugar cause diabetes?
Eating sugar alone doesn't directly cause diabetes, though diets high in sugar can contribute to weight gain, which is a risk factor for type 2 diabetes.

Can I still eat carbohydrates if I have diabetes?
Yes. Carbohydrates aren't off-limits — many care plans focus on the amount and type of carbohydrate eaten, and pairing carbs with protein or fiber to slow blood sugar spikes.

Is it safe to exercise with diabetes?
For most people, yes — exercise is generally recommended as part of diabetes management, though people on insulin or certain medications should talk to their care team about adjusting doses around activity to avoid hypoglycemiaHypoglycemiaBlood sugar that drops too low, which can cause shakiness, confusion, or in severe cases, loss of consciousness.
See full glossary →.

Quick Glossary

  • Insulin — a hormone made by the pancreas that helps move sugar from the blood into cells for energy
  • Beta cells — cells in the pancreas that produce insulin
  • A1C — a blood test reflecting average blood sugar over roughly three months
  • Hypoglycemia — blood sugar that is too low
  • Hyperglycemia — blood sugar that is too high
  • Insulin resistance — when cells respond poorly to insulin, requiring more of it to manage blood sugar
  • Prediabetes — blood sugar higher than normal but not yet at type 2 diabetes levels
  • Gestational diabetes — diabetes that develops during pregnancy
  • Ketoacidosis — a serious complication, most often in type 1 diabetes, caused by a severe lack of insulin
  • Neuropathy — nerve damage caused by prolonged high blood sugar
  • Diabetic retinopathy — eye damage from high blood sugar that can affect vision
  • Insulin pump — a wearable device that delivers a steady, adjustable flow of insulin
  • Continuous glucose monitor (CGM) — a wearable sensor that tracks blood sugar continuously
  • Carbohydrate counting — tracking carb grams eaten to match food to insulin or activity
